# Global and Indian school, college and university management software market

**Business strategy brief — information checked 27 November 2026.** The category includes student information systems (SIS), school management information systems (MIS), school ERP and higher-education campus ERP. Market-size definitions are not harmonized: some firms include broad education ERP, services and finance, while others isolate SIS software. The figures below are therefore directional market-sizing benchmarks, not additive totals.

## Market size and growth

| Research firm | Scope and publication year | Base-year estimate | Forecast | Reported CAGR |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Research and Markets / OG Analysis | Global school-management software, October 2025 | **$17.0bn in 2025** | **$83.8bn in 2034** | **19.4% (2025–34)** |
| Mordor Intelligence | Global education ERP, page updated August 2025 | **$24.24bn in 2025** | **$46.58bn in 2030** | **13.96% (2025–30)** |
| Fortune Business Insights | Global student information systems, page current in 2026 | **$17.88bn in 2025** | **$67.12bn in 2034** | **15.83% (2026–34)** |
| Strategic Market Research | Global SIS, published August 2025; 2024 base | **$9.8bn in 2024** | **$16.1bn in 2030** | **8.6% (2024–30)** |

The spread is material. It reflects different product boundaries more than a single “correct” number. Research and Markets describes an expansive school-management category that includes administration, academic workflows and communications. Mordor covers education ERP across K–12, higher education and vocational institutions; it says cloud held 63.85% of 2024 ERP revenue and that student information and administration represented 34.48% of the market. Fortune isolates SIS and says higher education represented 55% of 2025 SIS revenue. Strategic Market Research is narrower again and gives the lowest forecast. [1] [2] [3] [4]

The strategic signal is consistent even where the denominators differ: institutions are moving from legacy/on-premise systems toward cloud, integrated records, analytics, mobile access and AI-assisted workflows. The commercial opportunity is therefore a platform-and-integration market, not merely a database replacement cycle. The fastest growth should be in products that reduce reconciliation across admissions, student records, finance, attendance, learning tools, communications and reporting while remaining affordable to smaller schools.

UDISE+ 2024–25 is the latest accessed school report. It records 1,471,473 schools and 246.93 million enrolments from pre-primary through higher secondary, including 1,013,322 government schools, 79,349 government-aided schools and 339,583 private unaided recognized schools; the national total includes recognized madrasas and other schools. It also records 10,122,420 teachers. [5]

AISHE’s latest final report is 2021–22, rather than a 2023–24 or 2024–25 census. It registered 1,168 universities, 45,473 colleges and 12,002 stand-alone institutions. The estimated 43.27 million higher-education enrolment splits into 9.64 million in universities and constituent units, 31.46 million in colleges and 2.17 million in stand-alone institutions. These are different universes from UDISE+ and should not be summed as if school and higher-education records were one annual census. [6] [7]

For a vendor, India is a very large but heterogeneous addressable base. Government schools dominate institution count, while private schools and private higher-education institutions are more immediately reachable through paid procurement. The product has to work for large urban chains and universities as well as single, low-IT-capacity schools. That favors offline-tolerant workflows, low bandwidth, multilingual/vernacular interfaces, low implementation burden and pricing that can be quoted either per student or per school.

### Global counts from UNESCO UIS

UNESCO’s 2026 Global Education Monitoring release reports **1.4 billion students enrolled in primary and secondary education worldwide in 2024**. Its separate Higher Education Global Trends release reports **269 million higher-education students worldwide in 2024**, up from about 100 million in 2000. [8] [9]

The UIS Data Browser is the authoritative current system for country and regional education indicators. It says its November 2026 education release contained 9,737,492 data points across 5,335 indicators, and its background document says the formal-education survey covers public and private institutions from early childhood through tertiary education and collects institution, student and teacher data from countries. [10] [11]

The accessed UIS pages do **not** publish one consolidated headline total for the number of schools, colleges and universities worldwide. They provide downloadable country/region-level indicators, with coverage and reporting years varying by indicator. Accordingly, this brief reports the verified global learner totals above and does not invent a global institution count. A strategy team needing a global institution denominator should export the relevant UIS indicator from the Data Browser on the same date as the analysis and preserve the filter, year and coverage metadata.

## Consolidation, private equity and strategic transactions

Consolidation is moving the category toward broader suites and deeper data ownership.

* **PowerSchool/Bain Capital.** On 7 June 2024, Bain announced a transaction valuing PowerSchool at **$5.6 billion**. PowerSchool said it supported more than 55 million students and more than 17,000 customers in more than 90 countries at announcement. The transaction took PowerSchool private; Vista Equity Partners and Onex retained minority investments. [12]
* **Anthology/Ellucian.** Ellucian completed its acquisition of Anthology’s SIS and ERP business on 31 December 2025, announced 5 January 2026. More than 260 Anthology SIS/ERP customers joined Ellucian. The deal followed Anthology’s Chapter 11 process and is strategically important because it concentrates higher-education SIS/ERP customers under a vendor focused on higher education. [13]
* **Faria.** Faria’s 2026 press-release page records its acquisition of Edu Intelligence, an AI-native analytics platform for schools, announced 25 August 2026. Faria also acquired Vectare, a UK school-transport provider, in June 2025. These moves illustrate the suite strategy: combine core school records with transport, analytics and operational workflows rather than compete on SIS alone. [14]
* **ESS/SIMS and Montagu.** ESS was acquired from Capita by Tiger UK Bidco, a company established by funds advised by Montagu Private Equity, on 2 February 2021; the CMA-approved July 2021 announcement describes the intended merger with ParentPay and £45 million of deferred consideration to Capita. [37] The strategic fact for buyers is that SIMS has moved through ownership and product-modernization cycles while remaining a high-installed-base incumbent; ownership changes do not remove migration, contract and support risk for schools. No reliable 2026 purchase price was found, so none is stated.
* **Arbor and other platform groups.** Arbor’s growth has been accelerated by migration of The Key Group’s Integris and ScholarPack schools into Arbor, as well as wins from SIMS. This is consolidation through product retirement and internal migration as much as through headline M&A. [20] [21]

The implication for a challenger is that distribution, implementation capacity, migration tooling and an ecosystem are becoming as important as feature breadth. For incumbents, scale increases cross-sell potential but also increases blast radius when security or support fails.

## Security incidents and consequences

### PowerSchool, December 2024–2025

PowerSchool says it discovered unauthorized access and exfiltration from certain SIS environments through its PowerSource customer portal on 28 December 2024 and notified affected SIS customers on 7 January 2025. Potentially involved fields varied by customer and could include names, contact information, dates of birth, limited medical-alert information, Social Insurance Numbers, Social Security numbers and related information. PowerSchool offered two years of identity protection for affected students and educators and two years of credit monitoring for affected adults. The company said normal operations continued, so the main consequence was privacy, notification, regulatory and trust risk rather than prolonged service downtime. [15]

Reuters reported in May 2025 that multiple U.S. districts were later targeted in extortion attempts using previously stolen data. PowerSchool acknowledged paying an undisclosed ransom, saying it believed payment was in customers’ and communities’ best interests based on assurances and evidence that the data would be deleted. This illustrates why “the vendor paid and the service stayed up” is not an adequate risk metric: the downstream harm can be renewed extortion, monitoring costs, legal exposure and loss of confidence in a system of record. [16]

### Blackbaud, 2020

Blackbaud disclosed a ransomware attack in 2020 after attackers removed a copy of a subset of data from a self-hosted environment and the company paid a ransom. BBC reporting confirmed more than 20 universities and charities in the UK, United States and Canada as victims, with some data involving former students, staff, current students and supporters. Institutions had to notify affected people and faced criticism over the time taken to inform customers; the incident also raised GDPR’s 72-hour breach-notification questions. Reported data included phone numbers, donation history and event attendance, while payment-card details did not appear to be exposed in the cases described. [17]

### Illuminate Education, 2021 breach; FTC action in 2025

The FTC’s 1 December 2025 action says hackers used credentials of a former employee to access cloud databases in December 2021, reaching personal data for more than **10.1 million students**, including addresses, dates of birth, student records and health-related information. The FTC alleged weak access controls, threat detection, vulnerability management and plain-text storage, and said some districts were not notified for nearly two years. The proposed order requires a comprehensive information-security program, a data-retention schedule and deletion of unnecessary personal information, and bars misrepresentations about security and notification. [18]

### Frontline, August 2026

Frontline disclosed on 26 October 2026 that a third-party software vulnerability enabled unauthorized access to part of Frontline Central and School Health Management from 14–15 August 2026. It said impacted data could include Social Security numbers, dates of birth, driver’s-license or passport numbers, government IDs, Medicaid IDs and student IDs. Frontline notified affected districts and individuals and offered two years of credit monitoring for adults and cyber monitoring for minors. It said the incident was contained and it was not aware of misuse at the time of the notice. [19]

Across the incidents, the strategic requirements are clear: least-privilege access, rapid offboarding of staff, MFA and phishing-resistant authentication, encryption and key management, immutable audit logs, tested incident playbooks, tenant isolation, retention/deletion controls, transparent subprocessor governance, and contracts that define notification deadlines, forensic cooperation, data return and exit support. Security is not just a procurement checkbox: a school or university outsources operational trust when it outsources its student record.

## UK school MIS market-share shifts

The strongest public current evidence is DfE census analysis published by WhichMIS? and summarized by Finnemore Consulting. It is not an official DfE market-share table, so shares should be treated as industry analysis of DfE census returns rather than a DfE endorsement.

| Market and census | Arbor | Bromcom | SIMS | Other / context |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| England, May 2025 census analysis | **39%** | **15%** | **33%** | The Key survey’s stated sector shares |
| England, May 2026 census analysis | **11,787 schools / 53.58%** | **3,872 / 17.60%** | **5,782 / 26.28%** | SIMS was above 18,000 English schools and over 85% roughly a decade earlier |
| Entire UK, May 2026 analysis | **44.69%** | **20.50%** | **22.73%** | Wales and Northern Ireland procurement materially change the UK picture |

WhichMIS? attributes Arbor’s May 2026 gain of 823 English schools partly to 185 Integris and 160 ScholarPack schools moving within The Key Group, with a further 478 schools reportedly coming mostly from SIMS. Bromcom gained 282 English schools. Northern Ireland’s 1,116-school rollout gives Bromcom a major UK-wide boost, while Scotland’s SEEMiS remains used by all state schools but authorities are exploring alternatives. [20]

Finnemore’s September 2026 analysis describes Arbor as having crossed half of the English market, Bromcom as continuing to grow and SIMS as modernizing through Next Gen. It also notes that more than 4,000 schools switched MIS in 2025 and that the next competitive phase may be about retention rather than simply harvesting SIMS churn. [21]

iSAMS is important but sits in a different segment. Its own October 2025 announcement says it served more than 1,700 schools in over 90 countries, with particular strength in independent and international schools. That footprint should not be compared directly with the English state-school census shares above. [23]

The practical lesson is that cloud usability, migration execution, data portability, integrations, mobile access and change management are reshaping the market. The Key’s 2025 survey of 961 MIS users found average satisfaction of 6.6/10; only 47% were satisfied or extremely satisfied with intuitiveness for new users, 54% with support, 56% with actionable information and 47% with update frequency. Functionality was the top selection factor at 50%, useful feature range was 17%, integration 12% and price 10%. Among users of Arbor, SIMS and Bromcom, 21% overall were considering a change in the next year, rising to 35% among SIMS users. [22]

## AI adoption in administration

AI adoption is real but uneven, and “AI use” should not be confused with safe institutional deployment.

* RAND’s nationally representative 2025 K–12 survey found that 53% of ELA, math and science teachers used AI for school and 54% of students used AI for school. Only 45% of principals reported school or district AI policies, 35% of district leaders said they provided student AI training, and more than 80% of students said teachers had not explicitly taught them how to use AI for schoolwork. [24]
* Michigan Virtual’s 2026 survey found more than four in five educators had used AI personally and professionally. Among only 18 administrator respondents, 72.2% said AI was already in, or being added to, a district or school mission, vision or strategic plan, up from 46.8% in 2025 and 30.6% in 2024; the authors caution that the small administrator sample makes year-to-year comparisons fragile. [25]
* The most immediate administrative use cases are document intake and recordkeeping, parent and help-desk chat, translation and summarization, draft special-education documentation, natural-language reporting, attendance and behavior early-warning analysis, and workflow recommendations. EdSurge describes these as augmentation use cases and emphasizes privacy, training and equitable access. [26]
* UNESCO’s 2026 higher-education release says only one in five universities had a formal AI policy in 2025. That is a governance signal for campus ERP vendors: institutions may want AI assistants before they have data classifications, model-risk controls and accountable review processes. [9]

For product strategy, the safer AI wedge is permission-aware retrieval over trusted institutional data, with citations to source records, human approval for consequential actions, configurable retention and a complete audit trail. High-risk autonomous decisions about discipline, admissions, financial aid, safeguarding or disability support should not be marketed as “automation” without controls and evidence.

## What buyers want and where current systems hurt

### Desired capabilities

Across procurement documents, market pages, reviews and surveys, schools and higher-education institutions repeatedly want:

1. **A usable system of record.** Admissions, enrollment, attendance, grades, assessment, behavior, safeguarding, fees, finance, HR and documents should share identifiers and workflows rather than require duplicate entry.
2. **Actionable reporting.** Leaders want live dashboards, statutory reporting, custom report builders, longitudinal cohort views and early-warning signals, not merely data storage. EdSurge’s interoperability case describes the value of live gradebook and cross-system dashboards, standard data models and less time spent producing reports. [27]
3. **Open integration and portability.** Buyers want documented APIs, OneRoster/LTI or equivalent standards, SSO, data export, data dictionaries and clean offboarding. Integration matters because schools already have LMS, payments, transport, library, assessment, HR, finance, safeguarding and communication tools.
4. **Mobile-first family and staff access.** Parents want one login for attendance, grades, notices, fees, forms, events and two-way communication; teachers want quick attendance, behavior and communication workflows on phones.
5. **Low-friction adoption.** Clear navigation, role-based views, contextual help, sandbox training, migration validation and responsive support matter as much as feature checklists.
6. **India-ready inclusion.** Offline or store-and-forward operation, low-bandwidth performance, Android-first access, SMS/WhatsApp fallbacks, multilingual and vernacular interfaces, configurable local academic structures, digital payments and support for biometric/RFID/QR attendance are likely to matter more than heavy enterprise customization.
7. **Security and governance by default.** Schools want encryption, MFA, granular permissions, audit trails, backup/restore, retention controls, incident transparency and data residency options without having to bolt them on later.
8. **Safe AI.** Natural-language search over authorized data, report drafting, translation, summarization, anomaly detection and workflow suggestions are attractive when provenance, privacy and human review are built in.

### Common pain points evidenced in reviews and sector reporting

**Complexity and poor usability.** G2’s K–12 SIS category listed 386 products when accessed in November 2026, illustrating a fragmented buying market. [31] G2’s PowerSchool SIS page, updated in November 2026, shows 742 reviews and recurring themes of learning difficulty, poor usability and complex yearly tasks. Reviewers describe unintuitive reports and queries, an outdated UI and performance problems. [28] Blackbaud’s G2 page shows a 4.2/5 score from 29 reviews, but its AI summary still identifies learning difficulty, poor support, gradebook technical issues, inefficient updates and limited reporting/communication as recurring disadvantages. [29]

**Support and implementation burden.** The Key found only 54% positive satisfaction with support across its 2025 sample, with Bromcom at 42%, SIMS at 52% and Arbor at 63%. G2 reviewers also describe escalation and long response times. The market needs implementation partners, but a partner-heavy model can become another cost and dependency layer.

**Slow performance, dated interfaces and fragile modules.** Reviews mention outages, gradebook glitches, performance degradation at busy times and settings that do not persist. These are not cosmetic complaints: attendance, grading, enrollment and statutory reporting happen against deadlines, so latency or instability creates manual work and operational risk. [28] [29]

**Weak reporting and multiple disconnected tools.** EdSurge describes data silos when systems cannot interoperate. The positive case for Ed-Fi is revealing: standardization produced live views, data-quality checks, lower integration cost and faster reporting. The underlying pain is fragmented records and spreadsheets, not simply a missing dashboard. [27]

**Cost, price increases and total-cost opacity.** Buyers often compare a licence line while missing implementation, migration, training, servers, support, finance add-ons, SMS/WhatsApp, payment fees and exit costs. A Somerset Schools Forum paper records SIMS at £4.99 per pupil in 2022, a proposed 5% increase for 2022/23, anticipated SIMS contract prices from 2025/26 at least 25% higher, and separate server, maintenance, SQL migration and bolt-on costs. It explicitly frames the decision as total MIS-ecosystem cost rather than licence price. [34]

**Data lock-in and migration pain.** The Somerset paper lists SQL/server migrations and third-party bolt-ons among on-premise costs. Bromcom’s 2025 public pricing document offers migration packages, charges for additional academic years and provides up to two SQL data copies at offboarding, with a fee for further copies. The commercial response from vendors shows that migration is both a buying incentive and a switching barrier. [33]

**Low parent engagement and mobile gaps.** A technically complete SIS can still fail if a parent cannot log in, must use several apps, receives late notifications or cannot access the product on a low-end phone. Capterra’s category page shows that buyers evaluate admissions, attendance, scheduling, customizable reports, records, mobile deployment and support together; the feature list itself reflects the expectation of one connected experience. [30]

**India-specific digital-divide and language needs.** India’s 1.47 million-school base includes schools with widely varying connectivity, staffing and IT capacity. A cloud-only, desktop-first workflow that assumes continuous broadband and English-only administration will leave value on the table. UNESCO’s UIS data browser reports that, globally, only 50% of primary schools had internet access for teaching in 2025, illustrating why connectivity should be treated as a product constraint rather than an afterthought. [10]

| India | School ERP India public page: small-school plans **₹5,000/₹7,500/₹12,500 annually for 0–300 students**; custom tiers list **₹15,000 + ₹50/student**, **₹30,000 + ₹100/student**, and **₹90,000 + ₹300/student**, exclusive of 18% GST [36] | For 300 students, small plans are roughly **₹17–₹42/student/year before GST**, while the custom page lists ₹50/₹100/₹300 per student in its tier descriptions | This is a vendor page, and the exact recurring-versus-one-time treatment of custom per-student amounts should be verified in a quote. The important pricing comparison is not currency conversion. It is cost architecture. US enterprise systems often quote by institution, module and implementation; UK MIS procurement exposes base-plus-pupil charges; Indian providers commonly use low flat annual plans, per-student tiers, local integrations and optional maintenance. A product entering India should offer a transparent school-level plan, a per-student plan for chains and universities, offline/mobile capability in the core tier, and an explicit migration/exit promise.

## Strategic implications

The market is attractive but fragmented. The largest vendors are consolidating around system-of-record positions, while cloud-native challengers win through usability, migration and mobile experiences. The white space is not another long feature list. It is a trustworthy operating layer that makes data portable, reporting understandable, family engagement simple and administrative work measurably lighter.

A differentiated platform should lead with five proof points: fast time to value; audited, reversible data portability; open APIs and standard identifiers; resilient Android/offline/low-bandwidth operation with vernacular support; and permission-aware AI that cites records and leaves humans in control. Security should be designed as a product promise, not a procurement appendix. The incidents reviewed here show why a vendor that cannot explain access, retention, notification and recovery will increasingly lose trust even if its functional breadth is strong.
